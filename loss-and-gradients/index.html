Chapter 2

Loss and gradients: walking downhill

Training a network means finding the weights that make the loss smallest. This chapter turns that search into a picture: a landscape, and a ball rolling downhill.

Skip to the playground ↓ or read the introduction below

What this is

In this film, a neuron with just two weights learns to separate two groups of dots. On the right is every possible setting of its two weights, colored by loss. Training is the ball rolling downhill.

Chapter 1 described training as guess, measure, adjust. This chapter is about the measure and the adjust: what the loss is, why it looks the way it does, and how the gradient tells each weight which way to move. Chapter 3 then shows how backpropagation computes that gradient for a big network.

Why not just count mistakes?

The obvious way to score a network is accuracy, the share of dots it gets right. So why does chapter 1 train on a formula with a logarithm in it? Drag across the charts below. They show what happens to accuracy and to the loss as one weight, w₁, changes while the other stays fixed.

Accuracy only changes when the line jumps past a dot. Between jumps it is perfectly flat, so nudging a weight usually tells you nothing at all. The loss changes smoothly everywhere: every nudge says whether things got better or worse, and by how much. That is why networks are trained on a loss and judged on accuracy.

What the loss measures

For one dot, the loss looks only at the probability the network gave to the right answer. If the dot is blue, the loss is −log ŷ, where ŷ is the network's estimate that the dot is blue. Drag the network's confidence and compare it with the simpler alternative, squared error.

Three things make cross-entropy a good penalty. It is close to 0 for a confident right answer. It is 0.69 for a 50-50 shrug. And it grows without limit for a confident wrong answer. Squared error never goes above 1, and together with σ its push almost vanishes exactly when the network is confidently wrong, so the network learns slowly from its worst mistakes. The network's total loss is the average over all its dots.

The same thing in formulas
L = −[y·log ŷ + (1−y)·log(1−ŷ)]cross-entropy for one dot, y = 1 for blue
L = (ŷ − y)²squared error for one dot
∂L/∂z = ŷ − ycross-entropy's push on the neuron's sum z
∂L/∂z = 2(ŷ − y)·ŷ(1 − ŷ)squared error's push: the ŷ(1 − ŷ) factor shrinks to 0 when ŷ is near 0 or 1

The loss landscape

Now give the neuron two weights, w₁ and w₂, so it computes ŷ = σ(w₁x₁ + w₂x₂). Every pair of values is one point on a flat map, and every point has a loss: the average over all 160 dots. Lift every point to a height equal to its loss, and the map becomes ground, with hills where the neuron does badly and a valley where it does well. The best weights sit at the bottom of the valley.

The loss curve you dragged across earlier is one slice through this ground, cut along w₁. And the flat, colored map is the same ground seen from directly above, the way a hiking map shows height with color and contour lines. The film opens from above and then tilts, so you can watch one turn into the other.

The point on the map and the neuron are the same thing seen two ways. The point (w₁, w₂) is also an arrow from the center of the dot plot, and the neuron's line always runs at right angles to that arrow. Move the point and the line turns.

The gradient: which way is down

Standing at one point, the network cannot see the valley. It can only feel the slope under its feet, like walking downhill in fog. It measures the slope one weight at a time: nudge w₁ and see how the loss changes, then nudge w₂. Those two slopes together are the gradient. Drawn on the map, the gradient is an arrow pointing straight uphill, and its length says how steep the ground is. Training repeats three steps:

  1. Measure the gradient where you stand.
  2. Step the opposite way: w ← w − η × gradient. The learning rate η sets the size of the step.
  3. Repeat until the ground is flat.

This is called gradient descent. With 17 weights the map has 17 dimensions and nobody can draw it, but the rules are the same: the gradient is just a list of 17 slopes. Measuring them by nudging costs one run of the network per weight. Chapter 3 shows how backpropagation gets all of them at once.

How to read the figures

3D view
The floor holds every pair of weights, w₁ one way and w₂ the other. The height of the ground above each point is the loss for those weights. Its color repeats the height, deeper for lower, and the shading is light falling from one side, so slopes are easy to see.
Map view
The same ground seen from directly above: w₁ across, w₂ up, color for height. The thin lines join points of equal loss, like contour lines on a hiking map.
Ball and trail
The ball is the current weights. The trail is the path gradient descent took to get there.
Arrows
The dashed arrow is the gradient, pointing uphill; longer means steeper. The solid arrow is the step the ball will take: downhill, η times the gradient.
The dots
The neuron's line for the current weights, with the shading showing its guess, blue for blue. Hollow dots are on the wrong side. The arrow from the center is the point (w₁, w₂) itself.
Colors
Blue and orange keep their meaning from the series. Purple is new: it is the loss map, and deeper always means better.

Things to try

  • Drop the ball in a pale corner and press Run with η = 1. Watch the line on the left swing into place as the ball rolls.
  • Set η to 15 and drop the ball in the upper part of the valley. It bounces from side to side forever, because every step overshoots the floor.
  • From a pale corner, try η = 10. The first step is so big the ball flies off the map, then has to find its way back.
  • Switch to Accuracy and turn the 3D view. The ground is a staircase of flat terraces. Press Step: the ball cannot move, because the slope is zero everywhere.
  • Switch to Squared error and look for the high, flat plateau. Drop the ball on it: it crawls, because the push vanishes when the neuron is confidently wrong.
  • Look at the bottom of the valley. Even the best weights get some dots wrong, because no straight line through the middle can separate these two groups perfectly.

What runs where

Everything runs in your browser, with no graphics library. Each map is computed by evaluating the loss at 110 × 110 pairs of weights over all 160 dots, and the 3D ground is a 41 × 41 mesh of the same loss, drawn from back to front. The ball's gradient comes from the same autograd as the other chapters. The explanation box also shows the gradient measured by nudging each weight, so you can see the two agree.
