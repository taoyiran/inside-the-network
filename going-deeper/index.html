Chapter 4

Going deeper: why layers help

One hidden layer builds its guess by adding up soft straight edges. Stack layers, and each one gives every dot new coordinates for the next, so a network builds curves out of lines and shapes out of curves.

Skip to the playground ↓ or read the introduction below

What this is

This film races two small networks on four rings of dots, then opens one up to show what its layers do.

Chapter 1's network had a single hidden layer: each neuron drew one straight edge, and the output added the edges up. This chapter asks what changes when you stack a second layer on top, and what that costs.

The question

Here are four rings of dots, alternating blue and orange. Two networks race on them. One has a single hidden layer of 32 neurons, 129 parameters in all. The other has two hidden layers of 8 neurons each: 105 parameters, fewer than the first. Same dots, same kind of starting weights, the same learning rate η = 1, and 3,000 steps of gradient descent each.

After 3,000 steps the single layer still has parts of the rings wrong; the two layers get every dot right in about 1,500 steps. That is not the luck of one draw. We ran both networks from 20 different random starts. Within 3,000 steps the single layer of 32 never reached 95% (its best was 91%), while the two layers of 8 reached 95% in 19 of the 20 runs. Given many more steps the single layer does get there: it reached 95% in all 20 runs, but needed a median of about 5,700 steps, nearly four times the two layers' 1,500. In principle, one hidden layer with enough neurons can approximate any boundary. In practice, stacking layers often makes shapes like these much faster to learn, with fewer parameters. Two ideas explain why, and a third shows how such a network is trained.

Tool 1: a layer gives every dot new coordinates

A layer takes each dot's numbers and hands the next layer new ones: one number per neuron. When a layer has exactly two neurons, its two numbers can be drawn as coordinates on a new plane. This network, with layers of 8, 8 and 2 neurons, was trained on chapter 1's Spiral. On the left are the dots where they start. On the right, the same dots at their coordinates in the last hidden layer, the two-neuron one. Drag the slider to watch them move from one to the other.

In the new coordinates the two arms no longer wind around each other, and the output neuron separates the colors with one straight line. That is what the layers before it did: they bent and folded the plane until a straight cut was enough. The faint grid shows the bending: straight lines on the left come out bent and squeezed on the right. tanh never goes past −1 or 1, so much of the grid is pressed against the edges.

Tool 2: lines, then curves, then shapes

Each layer builds on the one below it. Here is the two-layer network from the race after training. Every square shows one neuron's output across the whole plane, blue for positive and orange for negative.

The first layer can only draw straight edges, exactly like chapter 1's neurons. The second layer combines them: its neurons respond to regions with curved edges, such as bulges, bands and arcs. The output combines those regions into one shape that puts every ring on its correct side. Lines, then curves, then the shape.

The price of depth

Depth has a cost, and part 4 above shows where it comes from. On the way back, the error is multiplied by the slope of every bend it passes. With many layers those slopes multiply together. σ's slope is never more than 0.25, so on its way back through six σ layers the slopes alone multiply the error by at most 0.25⁶, about 1/4,000; the weights can shrink it further. This widget measures it on fresh networks with six hidden layers of six neurons, at the very start of training, averaged over 20 random starting weights.

This is called the vanishing-gradient problem, and σ is the worst case. tanh's slope reaches 1 near zero, and ReLU's slope is exactly 1 wherever a neuron is active, so with well-scaled starting weights both keep the first layer's gradients within a small factor of the output layer's at this depth, as the widget shows. That is why hidden layers today use ReLU or close relatives of it rather than σ, and why starting weights are scaled with care: in the widget, starting weights half as big make every layer's gradient shrink, with any of the three bends, and the first layer's falls further behind the output layer's.

How to read the figures

Squares
Each square is one neuron's output for every point of the plane, on the same axes as the dots. Blue is positive, orange negative. The output square is the network's whole guess.
The dots
The shading behind the dots is the network's guess, deeper for more confident. The dark curve is the boundary, where the guess is 50%. Hollow dots are on the wrong side.
New coordinates
For a layer with two neurons: each dot is drawn at the layer's two numbers instead of its own x₁ and x₂. The straight line is where the output neuron's guess is 50%, and the faint grid is the original plane's grid, bent the same way.
Bars
The average size of the gradient for each layer's weights, on a logarithmic scale: each gridline down the axis is ten times smaller than the one above it. The values are averaged over 20 random starting weights.

Things to try

  • In the playground, train 1 × 32 on Rings, then 2 × 8. The two narrow layers usually pass 95% within about 2,000 steps. The single wide layer gets there too, but typically needs about four times as many.
  • In chapter 1, one hidden layer struggled with Spiral. Choose Spiral, set a single hidden layer of 8 (press − layer), and train; then try 8 → 8. In our tests from 20 starts, 8 → 8 got every dot right within about 3,200 steps every time, while one layer of 8 never did within 6,000.
  • Choose 8 → 8 → 2 on Spiral and watch the plane the last layer makes while it trains: the arms slowly come apart until one straight line separates them. This network is hard to train, because two neurons is a tight squeeze. From most starting weights it gets stuck with the arms still tangled: in our tests, only 4 of 20 starts passed 95% within 3,000 steps, while 8 → 8 without the two-neuron layer got every dot right from all 20. The preset begins from one that works; press Reset to see how other starts fare.
  • On Checkerboard, compare 2 × 8 with 3 × 8. In our tests from 20 starts, the third layer reached 95% sooner in 16 of them, typically in about 1,200 steps instead of about 1,700. Some starts go the other way, so try a few with Reset.
  • Switch the activation to ReLU and look at the boundary. It is made of straight pieces: before σ, a ReLU network computes a patchwork of flat, tilted pieces stitched together, so the line where it crosses zero is made of straight segments.
  • In the price-of-depth widget, compare σ with tanh and ReLU, then halve the starting weights.

What runs where

Everything runs in your browser, with no machine-learning library. This chapter trains with the layer-by-layer backward pass shown above, which is faster than chapter 3's operation-by-operation autograd for these bigger networks. When this page loaded it checked the two against each other on a network with three hidden layers: the check is running… The film, the race and the network in Tool 1 replay training that ran in your browser when the page loaded, from fixed random seeds. Browsers round a few math functions, such as ex, very slightly differently, and over thousands of steps those tiny differences grow, so exact numbers such as step counts and final accuracies can differ from one browser to another.
