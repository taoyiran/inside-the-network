Chapter 8

A tiny GPT, trained in your browser

Chapter 7's network had one layer of attention, so every key could say only what its own token was. A GPT stacks blocks of attention and a small MLP, and each block adds what it finds to every token's row, where the next block can read it. That makes possible something one layer cannot do: continue a pattern that the story itself set up, such as a made-up name. This page builds a GPT of 4 blocks, opens up what its heads learned, and lets you train one.

Skip to the playground ↓ or read the introduction below

The problem: continuing what the text started

A language model is mostly shown text it has never seen: a new name, a list, a variable in someone's code, a few examples of a task. A good one carries on in kind. It spells the name the same way each time, keeps the format of the list, uses the variable again. This is called in-context learning, learning from the text in front of it without changing a single weight. It is what made GPT-3 famous in 2020: shown a few examples of a task in its prompt, it could often do the task. Knowing what usually follows what, from training, is not enough for it, because whatever is new, by definition, never followed anything in training.

Why is it hard?

Chapter 7's attention can bring back a token from anywhere in the story, if its key matches what the reader is looking for. But a key is made from one token's row, so it can only say what that token is. Give a story's hero an invented name, “vorna”, cut into the pieces “v”, “##or” and “##na”. At a later mention, after “v”, the right next piece is the one that came after “v” before, so the network has to find a token by what came before it, and one layer of attention cannot express that. Two layers can: the first writes into each position which token came before it, and the second looks that up. But stacks of layers can be hard to train (in chapter 4, gradients faded over six layers of σ neurons, and needed well-scaled starting weights even with others), and the transformer's answer comes in two small parts, residual connections and layer norms. Here is a test story the networks never saw, with its hero renamed. Marked pieces come after the first piece of a later mention, where the story itself shows what comes next.

What a stack does differently

  • Every block adds to every row. A token's row passes up through the blocks, and each block adds what it found to the row instead of replacing it. This is the residual connection. So the row a later block reads still holds the token itself, plus everything the blocks below it added.
  • Later blocks read what earlier ones wrote. If a head in block 1 has written “the token before me was z” into a row, a key in block 2 can offer it. Heads in different blocks can work together, which is where the GPT's new abilities come from.
  • Layer norm keeps the numbers in range. Before each part reads a row, the row is rescaled to an average of 0 and a spread of 1, so each part sees numbers of the same size, however many blocks have added to the row.

How two blocks finish a name, step by step

Before the real network, a toy small enough to see every number. It has two heads, one in each of two blocks, and its numbers are made up: a token's row is a row of cells, one cell for each kind of token, so that a dot product of two of these rows is 1 when they name the same token and 0 when they do not. The arithmetic is real, computed as you watch. What the toy leaves out (the MLPs, layer norms, and rows of numbers that are not so tidy) the rest of the page covers.

That is the whole trick. A head in block 1 reads the token just before, by a high distance score at distance 1, and its result is added to each row, so every row now says what it is and what came before it. A head in block 2 asks, with its query, “whose previous token was the one I am?”, and compares that with keys made from the added part. It finds the token after the earlier copy and brings it back. Researchers who studied small transformers named the pair a previous-token head and an induction head. Without the residual connection, block 1's result would replace the row, and block 2's query would no longer know which token it is reading.

The question

Here is a short story whose first sentence names its hero. At its end the hero comes back, and the first piece of the name is already written. Type any name, or try the presets: which network finishes it?

The bars are each network's guesses for the piece right after the first piece of the name. Every name here is cut into pieces of chapter 6's vocabulary, so a name like “lily” is a single token and there is nothing to finish. The tools below take the GPT apart.

Tool 1: the residual stream

A token's row is 64 numbers, and it goes up through the 4 blocks. Each block adds to it twice: once with what attention brought back from earlier positions, and once with what the MLP made of the result. Nothing is replaced, so the row works like a shared notepad that every part reads and adds to; it is often called the residual stream. Type a phrase and follow its last token up the stack.

Each strip is drawn on its own scale, so compare colors within a strip, and sizes with the numbers beside it. The size of a row is its length: the square root of the sum of its numbers squared.

Tool 2: layer norm

Since every part adds to the rows, the rows can grow as they go up, and different numbers in a row can drift to very different sizes. Before attention and before the MLP, each block passes the row through a layer norm: subtract the row's average from each of its 64 numbers, divide by their spread (the standard deviation), then multiply each number by a learned gain and add a learned shift. The part then reads numbers of a steady size, whatever happened below it. The row itself, in the residual stream, is not changed; only the copy the part reads is.

Tool 3: a block, and the stack

Each of the 4 blocks has the same parts: a layer norm, attention with 4 heads (each with queries, keys and values of 16 numbers, and chapter 7's score for each distance back), a table Wo that turns the 4 heads' results back into a row of 64 so it can be added, a second layer norm, and an MLP of 256 neurons. After the last block, one more layer norm, and an output layer scores the 2,000 tokens. Type a phrase and see what comes out.

In all, 461,136 numbers to learn, 1.3 times chapter 7's 352,848. The output layer and the table take more than half of them, as in chapters 6 and 7; the 4 blocks take 203,008. Training is chapter 7's: the same loss, the same Adam, every position of whole stories at once, with 16 stories per step instead of 8 and a learning rate of 0.004 instead of 0.008. One thing in the data is new. In TinyStories, about two thirds of the stories have as their hero one of about two dozen names, such as “lily” or “tom”, each a single token. So each time such a story was drawn for a training step, with chance one half, its hero got a new made-up name, such as “nuto” or “gakobe”, built from random syllables, cut into pieces by the tokenizer, and put everywhere the name stands as a word, “tom's” included. The six invented names this page tests with, and every pair of their pieces, were never used. Secret 3 shows why this matters.

The words, and how they fit

Residual stream
The row of numbers each position carries up through the blocks. Every part reads it and adds to it, through a residual connection, so nothing written into it is lost unless a later part adds the opposite.
Layer norm
Rescaling a copy of each row to an average of 0 and a spread of 1, then a learned gain and shift for each number. Each block has two, one before attention and one before the MLP, and one more comes after the last block.
MLP
A small network applied to each position alone: 64 numbers in, 256 neurons, 64 numbers out, added to the row. Its neurons use GELU, a smooth relative of the ReLU: close to 0 for large negative inputs (never below −0.17), and about the input itself for large positive ones.
Block
Layer norm, multi-head attention, add; layer norm, MLP, add. Rows in, rows out, so blocks can be stacked.
Previous-token head
A head that reads mostly the token just before. Its result tells each position what came before it.
Induction head
A head in a later block that reads the position right after an earlier copy of the current token, and brings that token back: after “z”, what came after “z” before. It relies on a previous-token head below it, because the keys it matches are made from what the previous-token head added.
In-context learning
Doing better on text because of what came earlier in the same text, with no change to the weights. Finishing an invented name is a small case of it.
Logit lens
Reading a row partway up the stack as if it were the last: pass it through the last layer norm and the output layer, and see what the network would guess at that point. (A logit is a score before softmax.)
GPT
A generative pre-trained transformer: an embedding table, a way of marking positions, a stack of blocks with causal attention, a last layer norm and an output layer, trained to guess the next token. This page's is one, at a tiny size.

Secret 2: switch the helper off

Reading the right token does not prove a head is needed. To test it, switch a head off (its value table set to 0, so it adds nothing to the rows) and measure again. If the induction head relies on the previous-token head below it, switching off the previous-token head should break finishing names too, though that head reads mostly the token just before.

Secret 3: when it appeared

Induction heads are known for appearing suddenly. In small transformers trained on large amounts of text, there is a point early in training where they form and the network's use of earlier context jumps, often with a visible bump in the loss. Here is this GPT, measured at checkpoints saved during its training, next to the same network trained for as many steps on the stories as they are, without made-up names.

You can watch a jump like this happen in your browser. Below, a fresh network of 2 blocks with 2 heads each, attention only (no MLP neurons), trains on sequences of 8 to 24 different words drawn at random, each followed by the same words again. In the second copy, from its second word on, every word can be found by induction and by nothing else: the words are random and the length changes, so neither knowing words nor counting places helps. Press Train.

The grids show who each position reads in the test sequence below them: a row for each position that reads, a column for each position read, darker for more weight. A previous-token head draws a line just below the diagonal; an induction head draws a line parallel to it, in the second copy, one place to the right of the earlier copy.

Secret 4: what it thinks along the way

The residual stream can be read before the top. Take a token's row as it comes out of block 1, 2 or 3, pass it through the last layer norm and the output layer as if it were the last, and see what the network would guess at that point. Interpretability researchers call this the logit lens. It is not exact, because the output layer was trained to read only the top row, but it often shows a guess taking shape.

How to read the figures

Tokens
Each chip is one token, as in chapters 6 and 7. Pieces of one word sit glued together, the later ones starting with "##". "↵" is a line break, and "<end>" the token that opens every story. Orange marks an invented name. A chip outlined in black is the one reading; blue shading shows how much weight a head gives each earlier token, darker for more.
Blocks and heads
Blocks are numbered 1 to 4 from the bottom, the order in which a row passes through them; heads 1 to 4 within each block, in the order of the network's weights. Distance back counts tokens: 0 is the reading token itself, 1 the token just before.
Bars
Probabilities, or rates. The darkest bar is the top one; a blue bar marks the right answer.
Strips
A list of numbers drawn as cells: blue for a positive number, orange for a negative one, paper-colored for zero.

Things to try

What runs where

Everything runs in your browser, with no machine-learning library. The stories and the tokenizer are chapter 6's: 21,000 stories from the TinyStories dataset by Ronen Eldan and Yuanzhi Li (Microsoft Research), shared under the Community Data License Agreement – Sharing 1.0, 20,000 for training and 1,000 for testing; this page measures on the first 100 test stories. Chapter 6's and chapter 7's finished networks load from their chapters. The GPT was trained in advance with this page's code: … Its weights load from a file, rounded to 32-bit numbers to halve the download. The moments of training in Secret 3 were measured with checkpoints saved during that run and during a run without made-up names; the three other networks of Secret 5, and chapter 7's network retrained with made-up names (Secret 1), were trained the same way, and measured when this page was built. All of these are stored in the same file. Everything else runs live: the counting, the accuracy measurements, the invented names, the heads switched off, the logit lens, the live experiment, the training in the playground, and every guess. When this page loaded it checked the backward pass the slow way: the check is running… Browsers round a few math functions very slightly differently, so live training can come out a little differently from one browser to another.
