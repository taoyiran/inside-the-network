Chapter 7

Attention: looking back over the whole story

Chapter 6's network read the last 8 tokens, each in a slot of its own, so anything said earlier was gone. Attention lets every position look back over the story, here up to 256 tokens, and choose what to read: it compares what it is looking for with what each earlier token offers, and takes an average weighted toward the best matches. One layer of it, added to chapter 6's parts, remembers who a story is about.

Skip to the playground ↓ or read the introduction below

The problem: remembering the story

Read “Lily found a shiny shell on the beach. She put it in her pocket and ran home to show her mom. Her mom smiled and asked where she had found it.” and you know who comes next: Lily. Language keeps pointing back, to people and things introduced earlier and to what was said a while ago. A translation needs the whole sentence, an answer about a document needs the document, and a chat assistant has to keep track of the conversation. Attention was invented for this: first, in 2014, to let networks that translate look back at the whole sentence they are translating, and then, in 2017, as the core of the transformer, the design behind today's large language models.

Why is it hard?

Chapter 6's network read a window of the last 8 tokens, and making the window longer does not fix it. Every slot of the window has its own weights, 32 × 128 = 4,096 of them in the hidden layer, so a window of 256 tokens would need more than a million weights in that layer alone, and what one slot learns about names would not carry over to the others. Counting does even worse, because long stretches of text almost never repeat. Yet the word that comes next was often said long before. Here is a story the networks never saw. A word is marked when it already appeared earlier in the story, but not among the 8 tokens before it, out of chapter 6's reach; orange marks the story's name. (The 100 most common tokens, such as “the”, “she” or “.”, repeat all the time and are left unmarked, except a story's name.)

What attention does differently

  • It looks things up by content, not by place. Every token offers a short list of numbers, its key, and the position that is reading asks with a list of its own, its query. Tokens whose keys match the query get the most weight, wherever they are.
  • The same weights work at every distance. The weights that make queries and keys are shared by all positions, so reading 256 tokens back takes no more of them than reading 8. Only one small score per distance is added, so that order still counts.
  • Several lookups at once. The network makes 8 lookups side by side, its heads, and each learns to ask its own question.

Tool 1: a lookup by similarity

Every token gets three short lists of numbers, each made from its row of the embedding table by a small table of weights: a key, what it offers to be found by; a value, what it hands over when it is read; and a query, what it is looking for. To read, a position compares its query with the key of every token up to itself. The comparison is the dot product: multiply the two lists number by number and add up, which is large when they point the same way. Softmax turns the scores into weights that add up to 1, and the position takes the weighted average of the values. Queries and keys have 8 numbers each, values 16. Click any token to make it the one reading, and pick a head.

The scores are a soft version of looking something up. A dictionary finds the one entry whose key equals what you ask for; attention gives every entry some weight, more the better its key matches, and returns a blend. Because every step is a sum, a product or softmax, gradients flow through all of it, and the small tables that make queries, keys and values are learned like any other weights.

Tool 2: where, as well as what

A dot product of a query with a key says nothing about where the key sits: move the earlier tokens around and the weights move with them. But order matters (“the dog chased the cat” is not “the cat chased the dog”), so each head also learns a score for every distance back, from 0, the token itself, to 255, which is added to the dot product before softmax. A head with a high score at distance 1 reads the token just before, whatever it is. A head with flat scores reads by content alone. Here are the 8 heads' distance scores, as learned.

GPT-2 marks order differently: it adds a learned row for each position, 0 to 1,023, to the token's row. Scores that depend on how far apart two tokens are, as here, are closest to those of T5 (a learned score per distance, shared by groups of distances) and ALiBi (a fixed penalty growing with distance). Rotary position embeddings, used in many recent large models, also make the score depend on the distance, by turning queries and keys through angles that grow with position.

Tool 3: the network around it

The rest is chapter 6's network. The hidden layer reads the token's own row of 32 numbers and the 8 heads' results of 16 numbers each, 160 numbers in all; 128 tanh neurons read them, and an output layer scores the 2,000 tokens. Type a phrase and follow its last token through.

So the numbers the hidden layer reads for a token now depend on the story before it: the same token in two stories brings its own row, but the heads bring back different results. The token's own row is still the same in every story, but what the hidden layer reads alongside it now comes from anywhere in the window, chosen by content, rather than from 8 fixed slots. That is the start of the fix chapter 6 promised for “one word, one row”. In a single layer, these rebuilt numbers serve only to guess the next token; chapter 8 stacks layers, so that later ones can read them.

In all, 352,848 numbers to learn, about as many as chapter 6's 354,896: the attention network spends no more weights, but reads up to 256 tokens back instead of 8. Training is chapter 6's too: the same loss, −log of the probability given to the token that came next, and the same Adam. What changes is what a step reads. No position ever reads a later token, so one forward pass over a whole story makes a guess at every position at once, and the gradients of all of them are added up. Each training step reads 8 whole stories, about 2,000 positions, instead of chapter 6's 64 positions picked at random.

Secret 4: what one layer cannot do

Copying a whole name is one lookup: find a name earlier in the story and bring it back. Finishing a name made of pieces is two. Give a story's hero an invented name, such as “zarko”, cut into the pieces “z”, “##ark” and “##o”. At a later mention, after “z”, the right next piece is the one that came right after “z” before: the network must find the earlier “z” and then read the token after it.

How to read the figures

Tokens
Each chip is one token, as in chapter 6. Pieces of one word sit glued together, the later ones starting with "##". "↵" is a line break, and "<end>" the token that opens every story. A chip outlined in black is the one reading; blue shading shows how much weight it gives each earlier token, darker for more.
Heads
The 8 heads are numbered 1 to 8 in the order of the network's weights; the numbers mean nothing else. Distance back counts tokens: 0 is the reading token itself, 1 the token just before it.
Bars
Probabilities, or attention weights. The darkest bar is the top one; a blue bar marks the right answer.
Strips
A list of numbers drawn as cells: blue for a positive number, orange for a negative one, paper-colored for zero.
